Auditor (audit senior): I would like to understand how a project turns into revenue. Could you start with how a project is won and set up?

Project controller: A client accepts our fixed-fee proposal, and the commercial director signs it. Then I open the project in AFAS with the contract value and the budget hours agreed with the project lead.

Auditor: Who sets those budget hours?

Project controller: The project lead does, and I sanity-check them against the proposal.

Auditor: How is revenue recognised on a project?

Project controller: Over time, on percentage of completion. We take hours spent against the total budgeted hours and apply that to the contract value.

Auditor: So the numerator is hours booked. Where do those come from?

Project controller: Timewax. Everyone books their hours weekly and the project lead approves them. That feed comes into AFAS every Monday.

Auditor: And the denominator, the total budgeted hours. Does that change during a project?

Project controller: Yes, that is the part that moves. If the project lead thinks the job will take longer, he updates the estimate to complete and I put the new total in.

Auditor: Who reviews that new estimate before it goes in?

Project controller: Nobody formally. I would question it if it looked strange, but the project lead knows the job better than I do.

Auditor: How often are the estimates updated?

Project controller: Monthly in theory. In practice some leads update every month and some only when I chase them at quarter end.

Auditor: What happens to revenue if a lead increases the estimate to complete?

Project controller: The percentage drops, so we reverse some revenue that month. It happens.

Auditor: Is there a report showing which estimates changed and by how much?

Project controller: AFAS keeps the history, but I do not produce a report of it and nobody asks for one.

Auditor: Let me ask about the projects that are running at a loss.

Project controller: If the forecast cost exceeds the contract value we take the loss immediately. The finance director looks at that list each month.

Auditor: How would a loss-making project be identified?

Project controller: The margin report flags anything below zero. That one is reviewed properly, the finance director goes through it line by line.

Auditor: Now invoicing. When do you invoice a client?

Project controller: On the milestones in the contract. Typically thirty per cent on signature, then payments at design freeze and at delivery. So the invoice does not follow the revenue.

Auditor: Which means you have contract assets and liabilities.

Project controller: Yes. Where we have recognised more than we have invoiced it sits in accrued revenue, and where we have invoiced ahead it sits in deferred income. I prepare that schedule monthly.

Auditor: Who reviews that schedule?

Project controller: The finance director signs it off as part of the close.

Auditor: How thoroughly?

Project controller: He looks at the total and the biggest few projects. I would not say he reviews every line.

Auditor: What about variation orders, when the client asks for extra scope?

Project controller: The project lead agrees it with the client and sends me the email. I increase the contract value in AFAS from that.

Auditor: From an email. Is there a signed variation?

Project controller: Sometimes. For the bigger ones yes, there is a signed change order. For smaller extras it is often just the email trail.

Auditor: And you would recognise revenue on that increased contract value straight away?

Project controller: Once the contract value goes up, yes, the percentage applies to the new value.

Auditor: Let me turn to subcontractors. You said some work is subcontracted.

Project controller: About fifteen per cent of the work. Specialist structural calculations mostly. We contract with them, they invoice us, and we bill the client the full fee.

Auditor: Is that presented gross in revenue?

Project controller: Yes, gross. The subcontractor cost sits in cost of sales.

Auditor: Has anyone documented why gross rather than net?

Project controller: Not that I have seen. It has always been that way.

Auditor: How are the hours themselves controlled? Could someone book hours to the wrong project?

Project controller: They could, and it happens by accident. The project lead approves the weekly timesheets, which catches most of it.

Auditor: If hours moved from one project to another after the month end, would revenue change?

Project controller: Yes, it would move between projects. We do not lock the periods in Timewax until the quarter closes.

Auditor: So there is a window where prior-month hours can still be edited.

Project controller: There is, yes.

Auditor: Manual journals to revenue. Who can post those?

Project controller: I can, and the finance director can. Above fifty thousand euro AFAS requires a second approval.

Auditor: What kind of entries touch revenue at period end?

Project controller: The accrued and deferred movement, mostly. Occasionally a correction if a project was set up with the wrong contract value.

Auditor: Is anything tied to the reported result?

Project controller: The project leads have a bonus on project margin. The finance director and the commercial director have a profit-related bonus.

Auditor: How does management review revenue each month?

Project controller: The finance director reviews revenue by project against the forecast in the monthly pack, and the board see the total.

Auditor: What would make him challenge a number?

Project controller: A project where the percentage complete jumps a lot in one month, or where margin moves. He asks me and I explain it.

Auditor: Is that exchange recorded anywhere?

Project controller: In email only.

Auditor: Last one, access. Who can change the contract value or the budget hours in AFAS?

Project controller: I can, and the finance director. Project leads cannot, they can only submit the estimate to complete.
